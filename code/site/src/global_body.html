Global LEO filings

Before a country's operators can use radio frequencies for a satellite system, its national administration must file the system with the International Telecommunication Union (ITU). These filings are the earliest public record of planned satellites. This page maps every active ITU filing for a non-geostationary system with at least one orbit in low Earth orbit (perigee up to 2,000 km), by the country that filed it.

Data: ITU Space Networks System, up to BR IFIC No 3084 (10 Nov 2026) Pulled 13 Nov 2026 Scope: active non-geostationary filings with at least one LEO orbit
NOTE

ITU filings are not the same as FCC applications

This page counts ITU filings only. National applications, such as those made to the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC), are a separate step and are not counted until a matching ITU filing is recorded.

ITU filing: what this page counts

What it is. An international registration of a satellite system's radio frequencies and orbits with the International Telecommunication Union.

Who files. A national administration, on behalf of the operator. In the United States the government administration submits the filing (the FCC for commercial systems); an operator cannot file directly.

What it gives. International standing: a place in the queue for frequency coordination with other countries' systems, and, once recorded, protection from interference.

When it appears here. Once the ITU has received and recorded it. The ITU publishes filings in its fortnightly circulars, often some months after receipt.

National application (e.g. FCC): not counted

What it is. A request to one country's regulator for permission to launch, operate and use spectrum under that country's laws. Each country has its own regulator.

Who applies. The operator, directly. Applications are public in the United States and are often reported in the press as soon as they are lodged.

What it gives. A national licence. It does not by itself give any international standing; the administration must still file with the ITU, and the numbers in the two filings can differ.

Examples not on the map. SpaceX's FCC application of January 2026 for up to one million satellites, and Reflect Orbital's plan for up to 50,000 (its FCC application covers one test satellite). Neither had a matching ITU filing in the database as of 10 November 2026.

The leading filers and the stage they have reached

A filing passes through stages. Advance publication (API) announces a planned system; a coordination request (CR/C) starts negotiations with other operators and fixes the system's priority date; notification asks for the frequencies to be recorded in the Master International Frequency Register. Each bar counts filings by their current notice type.
